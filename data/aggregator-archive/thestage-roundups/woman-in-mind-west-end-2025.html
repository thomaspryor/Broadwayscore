Woman in Mind starring Sheridan Smith – review round-up

Review Round-UpsJan 8, 2026
Romesh Ranganathan and Sheridan Smith in Woman in Mind at the Duke of York's Theatre, London. Photo: Marc Brenner

She’s back. Three years after her acclaimed performance in Willy Russell’s Shirley Valentine, and two years since she starred in the ill-fated Opening Night, Sheridan Smith has returned to the West End, playing the lead role in Alan Ayckbourn’s Woman in Mind.

The prolific playwright’s 32nd work centres on Susan, an unhappily married woman who starts hallucinating an alternative life. It premiered at Scarborough’s Stephen Joseph Theatre in 1985, before transferring to the West End the following year.

Forty years later, and director Michael Longhurst’s revival is running at the Duke Of York’s Theatre until the end of February, with Tim McMullan, Louise Brealey and Sule Rimi appearing alongside Smith, plus comedian Romesh Ranganathan making his stage debut. After its London run, the production tours to the Sunderland Empire and Glasgow Theatre Royal.

Is Ayckbourn’s play still praised by the press four decades after its premiere? Is Smith showered with praise on her return to the West End? Do the critics mind Woman in Mind?

Fergus Morgan rounds up the reviews...

Romesh Ranganathan joins Sheridan Smith in Ayckbourn's Woman in Mind

Woman in Mind – a dated drama

Woman in Mind follows the fallout after fragile, middle-aged woman Susan is hit on the head by a rake and begins hallucinating an exciting alternative life, one in which her frustrating family and unfulfilling existence have been supplanted by a doting husband and a lavish lifestyle.

For some critics, it remains a potent play. It is “stunningly inventive” for Clive Davis (Times, ★★★★), “darkly comic” for Olivia Rook (LondonTheatre, ★★★★) and “a radical, form-bending exploration of psychiatric breakdown” for Claire Allfree (Telegraph, ★★★★). Blending “suburban comic pathos with a febrile dash of horror”, it is “one of Ayckbourn’s very best”, she adds.

Not everyone agrees. According to Andrzej Lukowksi (Time Out, ★★★), the play’s “lurid artifice and emotional corkscrews” make it feel like “a technical exercise”, while Alice Saville (Independent, ★★★) finds its depiction of mental health “frivolous” and “faintly tasteless”. Ayckbourn’s play contains “excruciatingly comic” and “supremely disturbing” moments, describes Nick Curtis (Standard, ★★★), but “concept overwhelms character”.

The drama is “choked off by formal conceit”, the “portrayal of psychological agony” is “crude and unconvincing” and characters are “flat and cartoonish”, contends Sam Marlowe (The Stage, ★★★). The play is “dated, blunt-edged and ultimately exasperating".

Woman in Mind – a surreal staging

Director Longhurst made his mark 14 years ago with his Royal Court production of Constellations, with subsequent successful productions including Amadeus at the National Theatre, Caroline, or Change at Chichester Festival Theatre, and Next To Normal at the Donmar Warehouse, where he served as artistic director for five years from 2019 until 2024.

His staging is largely lauded, with Curtis enjoying its “meta edge” and Rook admiring how it “cleverly balances the play’s surreal and real qualities”. Allfree hails a “triumphant” revival that manages to combine “Middle England sitcom” and “psychedelic fever dream”.

Longhurst’s creative team includes set designer Soutra Gilmour, lighting designer Lee Curran, sound designer Paul Arditti, and video designer Andrzej Goulding, all of whom are praised.

The show’s design is “brilliantly conceived”, writes Sarah Crompton (WhatsOnStage, ★★★). “The imagined family are dressed in outrageous, bright costumes, while the real favour woollens and browns. The garden where the action is set comes to warm life under Curran’s lighting whenever Susan is in her fantasy. It becomes dark and rain-filled as the action progresses, while Goulding’s video designs blur, alter and distort, suggesting various stages of her mental state.” The whole thing, notes Arifa Akbar (Guardian, ★★★), has “something of Oz”.

Sheridan Smith in Woman in Mind at the Duke of York's Theatre, London. Photo: Marc Brenner
Chris Jenks, Sule Rimi, Sheridan Smith and Safia Oakley Green. Photo: Marc Brenner

Woman in Mind – superb Smith

Smith started her career in the sitcom The Royle Family, before establishing herself as a powerhouse stage performer in Little Shop of Horrors and Legally Blonde. She pulled out of Funny Girl in 2016 due to stress and exhaustion, but bounced back in Shirley Valentine and Opening Night, despite the latter closing early after a critical drubbing.

Smith is showered with praise again for her performance here. As Susan, she is “tremendous” according to Allfree, “infinitely moving” according to Crompton, and “uniquely vivid” according to Curtis. “Smith packs a lifetime of disappointment, frustration and loneliness into Susan,” describes Marlowe. “As events turn more bizarre, Susan’s isolation and mounting panic bite, Smith clinging on desperately, watching in horror as all her certainties slide away.”

Elsewhere, there is plenty of praise for stage stalwarts McMullan and Brealey, and qualified approval of comedian Ranganathan, making his stage debut in the show.

As Susan’s dour vicar husband Gerald, McMullan is “exquisitely awful” for Allfree and “wonderfully pedestrian” for Saville, while Brealey’s portrayal of her sour sister-in-law Muriel is “comically miserable” for Crompton and “aggressively frumpy” for Allfree. Ranganathan, meanwhile, “aquits himself well” as Susan’s “bumbling, posh, repressed” doctor Bill.

Woman in Mind – the verdict

Everyone agrees that Sheridan Smith is excellent once again as Susan; that she is ably supported by Tim McMullan and Louise Brealey; that Romesh Ranganathan does a decent job in his stage debut; and that Michael Longhurst’s production has a disconcerting design.

The critics don’t concur over the play itself, though. For some, Alan Ayckbourn’s depiction of a depressed housewife losing her wits is darkly funny and dazzlingly clever. For others, its portrayal of mental health is dated, its characters thin, and its drama overwhelmed by concept.

Most award three stars, suggesting that this is a solid start to 2026, but not a spectacular one.
